Word Embeddings Explorer

How a single word becomes a list of numbers — and how those numbers encode meaning. A warm-up for Attention.

What's going on here?

A neural network can't read the word king — it needs numbers. An embedding is a lookup table that assigns every word a fixed-length list of numbers (a vector) such that words with similar meanings end up with similar numbers. This demo uses GloVe (Pennington, Socher & Manning, 2014), one of the best-known publicly available English word embeddings, trained by counting how often every pair of words co-occurs across billions of words of text and compressing those statistics down to 100 numbers per word.

Different models pick different vector lengths — GloVe ships in 50, 100, 200, and 300 dimensions; BERT uses 768; some smaller Transformer models use 512. The length itself isn't the point. What matters is the idea: meaning gets encoded as a position in a high-dimensional space, and every dimension of that space captures some (usually blurry, hard-to-name) aspect of usage. Search a word below, then look at the table — for each of its dimensions, you'll see which other words sit at the two extremes of that dimension, so you can get a feel for what it might be picking up on.

  • One vector per word, always — no context. GloVe gives bank exactly one vector, blending "river bank" and "money bank" into a single average point. That's precisely the limitation Attention fixes: it lets a model recompute a word's representation differently depending on the words around it.
  • No single dimension "means" one thing. Unlike a spreadsheet column, GloVe's dimensions aren't individually labeled ("dimension 12 = royalty"). Meaning is smeared across combinations of many dimensions at once — look for tendencies in the extreme-word lists, not clean categories.
  • Embeddings inherit bias from their training text. Famously, occupation words like doctor/nurse or engineer/homemaker pick up gender associations from the news and web text GloVe was trained on — a real-world consequence of "meaning" learned statistically rather than designed by hand.
  • This vocabulary is limited. To keep this page small enough to load in a browser, it ships the 25,000 most common words in GloVe's vocabulary (out of 400,000). A word that doesn't appear is "out of vocabulary" — a real problem production systems have to handle too (often by breaking rare words into smaller sub-word pieces).
