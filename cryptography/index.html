Caesar Cipher Lab

Turn a cipher wheel to hide a message — then use the statistics of the English language to break someone else's.

What's going on here?

The Caesar cipher is one of the oldest known ciphers — Julius Caesar reportedly used it for military messages around 50 BC. Every letter of the plaintext is replaced by the letter a fixed number of places further along the alphabet, wrapping from Z back around to A. That fixed number is the key (or shift). Caesar's own key was 3: A→D, B→E, … X→A.

The wheel below is a real cipher disk. The outer ring is the plaintext alphabet and stays put; the inner ring is the ciphertext alphabet. Drag the inner ring (or use the buttons, slider, or arrow keys) to change the key, and watch every letter's mapping change at once. Hover any letter to trace it.

  • Same key, both directions: to encrypt, read outer → inner. To decrypt, read inner → outer. Sender and receiver must share the key — this is symmetric encryption.
  • Only 25 useful keys: a key of 0 (or 26) changes nothing. That tiny key space is the cipher's first fatal weakness — you could just try them all.
  • The second weakness: every E always becomes the same letter. The cipher hides which letters you used, but not how often — the Cryptanalysis tab exploits exactly that.

Breaking the cipher without the key

Cryptanalysis is the art of reading a secret message without being given the key. Around 850 AD the Arab scholar al-Kindi described the technique you'll use here: frequency analysis. In any long piece of English, letters show up at predictable rates — E is about 12.7% of all letters, T about 9%, while Q, X, and Z barely appear at all.

A Caesar cipher shifts every letter by the same amount, so it shifts that whole pattern by the same amount too. The ciphertext's bar chart is just the English bar chart slid sideways. Find how far it slid, and you've found the key.

  1. Count how often each letter appears in the ciphertext.
  2. Compare that shape against normal English.
  3. Slide the English pattern until the peaks and valleys line up.
  4. Verify by decrypting — real words should appear.

Step 1 — Count the letters

The chart below counts every letter in the ciphertext (cyan bars) and puts normal English letter frequencies beside them (orange outlines). Right now your key guess is 0 — no shift — and the two clearly don't match: the ciphertext's peaks are in the wrong places.

Look at the English outlines: tall at E, T, A, O, I, N; nearly flat at J, Q, X, Z. Can you spot that same “skyline” somewhere in the cyan bars, just moved over?

Step 3 — Slide English until it lines up

One letter can be a coincidence — a whole pattern can't. Move the key guess and watch the orange outlines slide. When the guess is right, every peak lines up: E, T, A, O, and also the low J-K and Q-X-Z valleys. The mismatch score (a chi-squared statistic) measures how far apart the two shapes are — lower is better.

If “assume it's E” didn't produce English, the top letter might be T or A instead. Try a guess that makes the second-tallest bar line up with E.

Step 4 — Verify: does it read as English?

Statistics point to a likely key; reading the result proves it. Check the live decryption under the chart. Real words? You've broken the cipher.

Step 5 — Let the computer try every key

A computer doesn't need to eyeball anything: it scores all 26 keys in well under a millisecond and picks the lowest mismatch. Try it yourself first — then reveal its answer to check your work.

Why this matters: modern ciphers like AES are designed so ciphertext looks statistically random — every byte equally likely, no skyline to line up. A cipher that leaks the plaintext's patterns can be broken no matter how secret its key is.
